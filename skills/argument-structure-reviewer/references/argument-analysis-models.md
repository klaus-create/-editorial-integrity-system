# Argument Analysis Models

## Purpose

Use this guide to diagnose reasoning at multiple scales without turning every form into an academic essay or replacing the author’s position.

## 1. Basic argument map

Identify:

- thesis or decision;
- supporting claims;
- premises;
- evidence;
- warrants connecting evidence to claim;
- assumptions;
- counterclaims;
- conclusion or recommendation.

A missing explicit warrant is not always a defect. It becomes a defect when the reader cannot defensibly infer the bridge or when the bridge is contested.

## 2. Support sufficiency

Test each material claim for:

- relevance: evidence bears on this proposition;
- scope: evidence supports the strength and breadth of wording;
- quantity: enough evidence exists for the consequence;
- quality: sources are suitable for the claim type;
- independence: repeated claims do not merely echo one origin;
- recency: time-sensitive claims remain current;
- counterevidence: credible contrary material is addressed.

The Reviewer diagnoses sufficiency from supplied artefacts. It does not independently verify sources.

## 3. Causal chain analysis

Separate:

- temporal sequence;
- association;
- plausible mechanism;
- necessary condition;
- sufficient condition;
- contributing factor;
- direct cause.

Test:

1. Did the proposed cause precede the effect?
2. Is there a plausible mechanism?
3. Were relevant alternatives considered?
4. Does the evidence design support causal inference?
5. Does the wording match the evidence strength?

A recommendation can be sensible under uncertainty without a proven causal claim. Preserve that distinction.

## 4. Counterargument analysis

A credible counterargument should be:

- materially relevant to the thesis;
- represented fairly;
- answered with evidence or acknowledged limitation;
- proportionate to consequence.

Do not add performative balance where no credible alternative exists. Do not omit a material objection because it complicates the narrative.

## 5. Decision-usefulness model

For decision material, ask whether the piece makes visible:

- decision required;
- relevant evidence;
- assumptions;
- options or alternatives;
- constraints and dependencies;
- recommendation;
- owner and timing;
- risk of action and inaction;
- what would change the recommendation.

A logically coherent report can still be decision-useless if it withholds these elements.

## 6. Structural scales

### Sentence to paragraph

Does each sentence advance, support, qualify or transition the paragraph’s job?

### Paragraph to section

Does each paragraph perform a distinct function, or does the section circle the same point?

### Section to whole

Does the section fulfil a necessary step in the thesis, decision or narrative promise?

### Whole to larger work

For books, report series and presentations, does the piece contribute without duplicating or contradicting adjacent units?

## 7. Form-specific models

### Report

Question -> observation -> interpretation -> implication -> recommendation -> decision conditions.

### Proposal

Problem -> consequence -> value mechanism -> approach -> proof -> scope and decision.

### Presentation

Each slide has one idea job; sequence creates movement; evidence appears at the point of doubt; the conclusion is earned by prior slides.

### Article or essay

Framing -> developed thesis -> evidence/examples -> counterpoint -> conclusion. Scene or narrative may perform framing and evidence functions.

### Speech

Oral signposting, recurrence and emotional movement matter. Repetition may be structurally necessary because the audience cannot reread.

### Book chapter

Chapter promise -> internal movement -> manuscript contribution. Motifs and recurrence may operate across chapters.

## 8. Repetition test

Classify recurrence as:

- orientation;
- escalation;
- reinforcement;
- thematic motif;
- rhythmic device;
- summary;
- accidental duplication.

Only the last category is presumptively removable. Test whether deleting the recurrence damages memory, emphasis, structure or continuity.

## 9. Severity calibration

### P0

The central conclusion depends on a contradiction, impossible premise or invalid logical dependency.

### P1

A material claim lacks support, causal force exceeds evidence, sequence prevents comprehension, or conclusion materially overreaches.

### P2

A local bridge, paragraph function or counterpoint is underdeveloped but the core argument survives.

### P3

Optional improvement with no meaningful decision or comprehension consequence.

Severity describes consequence. Confidence describes certainty in the diagnosis.

## 10. Repair direction

A review may recommend:

- clarify the thesis;
- supply or remove a claim;
- state the warrant;
- weaken causal force;
- reorder units;
- add a credible counterargument;
- separate observation from interpretation;
- narrow the conclusion;
- return an accountable choice to the author.

Do not provide replacement prose unless a later editing step is authorised.
