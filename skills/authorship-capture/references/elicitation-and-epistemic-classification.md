# Elicitation and Epistemic Classification Guide

## Purpose

Use this guide when the source material is incomplete, conversational, contradictory or dependent on personal judgement. The objective is not to make the material coherent at any cost. It is to expose what the accountable author actually knows, believes, remembers, disputes and authorises.

## 1. Elicitation sequence

Ask or infer only from supplied material in this order:

1. **Reader outcome**: What must the reader understand, decide, feel or do?
2. **Governing proposition**: What is the author ultimately saying is true, important or required?
3. **Observed reality**: What happened or exists independently of the author’s evaluation?
4. **Interpretation**: What does the author believe the observations mean?
5. **Causality**: What does the author believe produced the result, and what evidence supports that relation?
6. **Experience**: What did the author personally see, do or feel?
7. **Evidence**: Which source supports each material proposition?
8. **Dissent**: Where does the author disagree with sources, stakeholders or conventional framing?
9. **Uncertainty**: What remains unknown, contested or provisional?
10. **Boundaries**: What must not be invented, disclosed, softened or overstated?

Do not ask for stylistic preferences until the substantive record is stable enough to separate meaning from expression.

## 2. Atomising claims

Split a sentence when its parts have different evidence burdens.

Example:

> The campaign increased qualified leads by 30 per cent and proved the new positioning resonated with the market.

Atomise into:

- leads increased by 30 per cent;
- the leads were qualified according to a defined criterion;
- the campaign produced the increase;
- the result demonstrates positioning resonance;
- the observed market is representative of the intended market.

These may require different sources and may receive different publication statuses.

## 3. Epistemic classification tests

### Verified fact

Use only when an approved source has already been checked for this assignment and directly supports the proposition within its scope.

### Supplied source

Use when the proposition appears in supplied material but has not been independently checked. “It is in the deck” is provenance, not verification.

### Attributed view

Use when a named person or organisation holds the position. Attribution does not make the view independently true.

### Author judgement

Use for strategic, ethical, aesthetic or evaluative conclusions owned by the author. Do not demand external proof for a genuine judgement, but do not present it as objective fact.

### Personal recollection

Use for memory of events or experience. Preserve first-person ownership and uncertainty. Recollection may be valuable without being independently verifiable.

### Inference

Use when the proposition follows from evidence through a reasoning step. Record the evidence and the inferential move.

### Contested

Use when credible sources or stakeholders conflict. Preserve the disagreement and define what would resolve it.

### Uncertain

Use when the author explicitly lacks confidence or the available record cannot support stronger wording.

### Unsupported

Use when the proposition is present but no authorised basis is available. Unsupported is not the same as false.

## 4. Publication status is separate

An item’s epistemic class does not determine its final treatment automatically.

- verified fact may still require qualification because its measured scope is narrow;
- author judgement may be approved if clearly owned;
- supplied source may require verification;
- contested evidence may be publishable with balanced attribution;
- unsupported material may be queried or removed;
- personal recollection may be approved in first person but not as a historical superlative.

## 5. Source inventory quality

For every source record:

- stable source ID;
- owner or publisher;
- source type;
- date or period;
- permission status;
- whether it is primary, secondary or recollection;
- limitations relevant to the claim;
- whether current verification is required.

Permission and reliability are different dimensions. A highly reliable confidential source may still be unusable for publication.

## 6. Contradiction handling

Classify contradictions:

- factual: sources disagree about what occurred;
- definitional: terms or denominators differ;
- temporal: statements refer to different periods;
- strategic: stakeholders prefer incompatible priorities;
- authorial: the author’s notes contain two incompatible theses;
- evidentiary: a conclusion exceeds the source.

Do not resolve by majority wording. Record the conflicting items, consequence, owner and resolution action.

## 7. Protected language

Protect wording only when it carries a substantive function such as:

- strategic precision;
- legal or technical meaning;
- identity, dialect or cultural context;
- emotional ownership;
- a deliberate conceptual distinction;
- a movement, product or framework term.

Do not protect a phrase merely because it is memorable. Record why changing it would alter meaning or authorship.

## 8. Sufficiency matrix

| Need | Sufficient | Conditional | Insufficient |
|---|---|---|---|
| Outline | governing question and major authorised themes | competing themes visible | topic only |
| Marked draft | provisional thesis, classified claims, explicit gaps | some non-core gaps | missing core substance or experience |
| Publication draft | authorised thesis, permissions, evidence duties, no blocking contradiction | not applicable without explicit release restriction | unsupported or unauthorised central claim |

## 9. Claim Registry parity

Every substantive item must have one corresponding Claim Registry entry with:

- the same stable ID;
- equivalent claim wording;
- compatible source status;
- source references that exist in the source inventory;
- verification requirement;
- publication status;
- final locations added later by downstream work.

The Source Pack and embedded Claim Registry must share assignment ID, completion status, unresolved issues and human-action flag.

## 10. Elicitation failure tests

- Am I asking the author to endorse language I generated rather than eliciting their thought?
- Did I merge disagreement into a false consensus?
- Did I convert a memory into a fact?
- Did I infer a motive, experience or example that was not supplied?
- Did I confuse confidence with evidence quality?
- Can a downstream agent distinguish what may be asserted, attributed, inferred, queried or omitted?
