# Voice Analysis and Sampling Guide

## Purpose

Use this guide when building a reusable voice profile. Voice is a pattern of decisions across contexts, not a list of adjectives, favourite words or copied phrases.

## 1. Sample design

A sample set should vary enough to reveal stable behaviour without mixing unknown authorship.

Record for each sample:

- stable sample ID;
- form and surface;
- audience and purpose where known;
- date;
- authorship confidence;
- editing history;
- inclusion or exclusion decision;
- limitation.

Exclude or sharply downgrade:

- generated text that was not materially human-edited;
- ghostwritten work with unknown authorship contribution;
- heavily edited work without access to the author’s accepted version;
- duplicated or near-duplicated pieces;
- text outside the intended profile scope.

## 2. Representativeness

A profile intended for multiple registers should include evidence from each register or explicitly state that the register is uncovered.

Do not infer:

- report behaviour from social captions;
- speech cadence from written articles;
- personal voice from institutional copy;
- enduring syntax from one unusually constrained legal or technical document.

## 3. Six analysis layers

### Rhetorical development

Observe where the governing point appears, how tension is introduced, how scope is narrowed, whether counterpoints appear, and how the reader is moved toward a conclusion.

### Syntax and cadence

Observe sentence-length distribution, clause layering, fragments, periodic versus direct syntax, paragraph rhythm and variation. Do not reduce cadence to an average sentence length.

### Lexical behaviour

Observe abstraction level, concrete verbs, technical terminology, qualification, metaphor, idiom and intensity. Distinguish domain-required language from author preference.

### Structure and recurrence

Observe section sequencing, list use, repetition, callbacks, asymmetry and how hierarchy is signalled. Repetition can be purposeful rather than redundant.

### Punctuation and surface

Observe dashes, colons, parentheses, sentence fragments, capitalisation and heading behaviour only when recurrent and functional. A punctuation mark alone is not a voice trait.

### Epistemic stance

Observe certainty, attribution, ownership of judgement, willingness to expose uncertainty, causal restraint and relation to evidence.

## 4. Candidate trait formulation

A useful trait contains:

1. an observable behaviour;
2. scope: enduring, register-specific, context-dependent or candidate;
3. evidence references;
4. counterevidence;
5. confidence with rationale;
6. a positive writing instruction;
7. an anti-pattern.

Weak: “confident and sophisticated”.

Operational: “States the governing claim in the opening paragraph, then narrows its boundary before moving to implications; avoid generic scene-setting that delays the proposition.”

## 5. Evidence thresholds

- enduring trait: at least two independent included samples, preferably across contexts;
- register-specific trait: at least two included samples within that register and counterevidence outside it;
- context-dependent trait: a clear condition explains variation;
- candidate: one or two indications without sufficient recurrence;
- rejected: evidence is attributable to topic, template, editor or formal constraint rather than voice.

A larger number of weak or derivative samples does not outperform a smaller set of confirmed human work.

## 6. Contrastive analysis

Compare the candidate trait against a generic baseline:

- Would competent writers in this form commonly do the same thing?
- Is the behaviour imposed by the format or template?
- Is the language required by the subject?
- Does the author choose the behaviour even when alternatives were available?
- Do accepted and rejected edits reveal a boundary?

Only behaviour that changes a writing or editing decision belongs in the profile.

## 7. Protected characteristics

Protect a characteristic when removing it would materially change:

- rhythm or emphasis;
- emotional distance;
- directness or restraint;
- cultural or dialectical identity;
- conceptual precision;
- deliberate asymmetry or recurrence.

Record the editing implication: preserve, query before changing, or restrict to named registers.

## 8. Confidence rubric

### High

Confirmed authorship, recurrent evidence, relevant register coverage, limited counterevidence, and accepted/rejected edit evidence.

### Moderate

Reasonable recurrence with incomplete provenance, narrow register coverage or meaningful counterevidence.

### Low

Sparse samples, uncertain editing history, behaviour dependent on one context or weak distinction from generic form conventions.

Do not express numerical precision that the evidence cannot support.

## 9. Imitation boundaries

The profile may describe high-level behaviour. It must not:

- store or reproduce signature phrases as reusable prompts;
- enable close imitation of a living public writer;
- infer personality or private identity from prose;
- extend beyond the owner’s permission;
- auto-learn from generated output merely because it was published.

## 10. Profile refresh

Refresh when:

- confirmed human samples expand register coverage;
- the owner explicitly accepts or rejects material edits;
- a new institutional governance rule changes permissible voice;
- evidence contradicts a high-confidence trait;
- ownership or editing provenance changes.

Version the profile and preserve superseded evidence rather than silently overwriting history.
