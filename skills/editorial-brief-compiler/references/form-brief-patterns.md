# Form Brief Patterns

## Purpose

Use this guide when the requested output form materially changes argument, density, pacing, evidence display or reader action. These are functional architectures, not rigid templates.

## 1. Universal brief distinctions

Keep these fields separate:

- **objective**: the work the piece must perform;
- **reader effect**: what changes for the reader;
- **thesis**: the governing proposition;
- **surface message**: the concise articulation the reader encounters;
- **call to action**: the next step requested;
- **release condition**: what must be completed before circulation.

A brief is incomplete when one field substitutes for another.

## 2. Claim and evidence matrix

For every material claim, record:

- claim ID;
- required publication status;
- evidence duty;
- acceptable qualification or attribution;
- location or structural role;
- verifier requirement;
- consequence if removed.

Do not retain a claim merely because it would make the piece more persuasive.

## 3. Email and direct message

Functional jobs:

1. orient the recipient only as much as necessary;
2. provide the answer, request or decision early;
3. state the reason or constraint;
4. specify owner, timing and next action;
5. preserve relational tone without obscuring the ask.

Brief for length in reader actions, not word count alone. A courteous preamble that delays an urgent decision is a structural defect.

## 4. Social and marketing copy

Define:

- specific reader tension or useful promise;
- authorised proof or experience;
- hook boundary: no invented urgency, false universality or formulaic uplift;
- platform constraint;
- desired interaction or action;
- relationship to the broader content system.

A hook is not the thesis. It earns attention for the authorised substance.

## 5. Strategic report

Functional sequence:

1. question or decision context;
2. observations and evidence;
3. interpretation;
4. implications and alternatives;
5. recommendation;
6. assumptions, risks, owner and next step.

Require explicit separation between observation, interpretation and recommendation when readers may otherwise treat judgement as data.

## 6. Proposal or business case

Define:

- client or organisational problem;
- consequence of inaction;
- proposed value and mechanism;
- scope and approach;
- evidence or proof;
- commercial or operational assumptions;
- risks and dependencies;
- decision requested.

Do not allow the solution to define the problem retrospectively.

## 7. Presentation

Brief at the slide level:

- one idea job per slide;
- reader question answered;
- claim and proof;
- visual job;
- speaker narrative versus on-slide text;
- transition to the next idea;
- appendix destination for necessary detail.

When the slide limit conflicts with content volume, require prioritisation rather than font reduction or prose compression.

## 8. Article or essay

Define:

- framing question;
- earned thesis;
- development sequence;
- evidence and examples;
- credible counterpoint;
- conclusion scope;
- desired reader reflection or action.

A scene-led opening is valid when it establishes the argument’s promise. Do not force the thesis into the first sentence mechanically.

## 9. Speech or script

Add constraints for:

- spoken duration;
- breath and sentence length;
- emphasis and repeatable lines;
- audience response moments;
- pronunciation and technical language;
- stage, camera or audio context;
- transitions that are audible without visual headings.

Writing that reads well silently may fail in delivery.

## 10. Book or chapter

Define three scales:

- manuscript contribution: why this chapter exists;
- chapter promise and internal movement;
- section-level jobs and recurrence.

Record continuity dependencies, motifs, concepts already established and ideas deliberately deferred. Do not remove recurrence without testing its manuscript-level function.

## 11. Constraint conflict

When constraints conflict, record:

- the two constraints;
- their authority level;
- what cannot be simultaneously satisfied;
- available trade-offs;
- accountable decision owner;
- effect on claims, form and release.

Typical conflicts:

- slide limit versus evidence volume;
- forceful register versus uncertain evidence;
- strict brevity versus required qualification;
- public transparency versus source permission;
- local voice preference versus institutional terminology.

## 12. Executability test

A downstream Drafter should be able to answer:

- What must this piece achieve?
- Which thesis is authorised?
- What may be claimed, inferred or attributed?
- What is prohibited?
- What structural job does each unit perform?
- Which voice behaviours apply here?
- What remains conditional?
- What must happen before release?

If these answers depend on hidden conversation, the brief is not portable.
